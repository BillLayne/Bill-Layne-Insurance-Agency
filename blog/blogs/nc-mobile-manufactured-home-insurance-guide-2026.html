North Carolina Mobile & Manufactured Home Insurance Guide (2026): Coverage, Tie-Downs & Best Carriers

How NC carriers classify your home, what underwriters look for on the lot, and which companies actually write it.

By Bill Layne, licensed NC agent since 2005Published October 16, 202611 min read

Quick answer

Mobile and manufactured home insurance in North Carolina (often called an HO-7 or MH policy) protects your single-wide, double-wide, or sectional home against fire, wind, hail, theft, and liability—whether it sits on private rural acreage or a leased park lot. Specialized NC carriers like Foremost and National General offer replacement cost, matching siding, and trip collision endorsements.

A lot of folks in Surry, Wilkes and Yadkin counties find out the hard way that their manufactured home doesn’t fit the box a regular homeowners quote is built for. They fill out a form online, get a decline or a strange number, and assume nobody will write it.

That usually isn’t true. Mobile home insurance in North Carolina is its own product, with its own carriers and its own underwriting rules. Once you know what those rules are, the shopping gets a lot easier.

How Does Mobile and Manufactured Home Insurance Work in North Carolina?

A standard HO-3 homeowners policy is written for site-built homes. Many HO-3 programs simply won’t accept a HUD-code manufactured home, because the construction, anchoring and loss history are different. So carriers built a separate form for it.

HO-7 / Manufactured Home (MH) policy
A homeowners-style policy written specifically for HUD-code manufactured and mobile homes. Names vary by carrier, but the structure mirrors a regular homeowners policy: coverage for the home, other structures, belongings, living expenses and liability.

Here’s how the core coverages line up on a typical NC manufactured home policy:

  • Coverage A — Dwelling. The home itself, including attached additions, built-in appliances and permanently attached porches.
  • Coverage B — Other Structures. Things not attached to the home: detached carports, decks that stand free of the home, storage sheds, workshops and well houses. On rural lots in the Foothills, that well house matters more than people think.
  • Coverage C — Personal Property. Furniture, clothes, electronics and tools. Often a set share of Coverage A unless you change it.
  • Coverage D — Loss of Use / Additional Living Expenses. Pays reasonable extra costs to live somewhere else while a covered loss is repaired.
  • Coverage E & F — Personal Liability and Medical Payments. Protects you if someone is hurt on your property or you accidentally damage someone else’s.

For the bigger picture on property coverage, start at our North Carolina homeowners and property insurance hub. If you’re wondering what the state actually requires, see North Carolina homeowners insurance laws and requirements. The short version: the law doesn’t require it, but a lender or a park lease usually does.

Single-Wide vs. Double-Wide vs. Modular Homes: How Do NC Carriers Classify Your Home?

Before a carrier prices anything, it decides what kind of home you have. Single-wide, double-wide or triple-wide is about width. The bigger question is which building code the home was built to.

Double-wides and sectionals often look like site-built homes once they’re set, skirted and landscaped. That doesn’t change the classification. If it has a HUD label, carriers treat it as a manufactured home.

Find your label before you call. The red HUD tag is usually on the back end of each section, near the tail light end. The data plate is often inside a kitchen cabinet, a bedroom closet or near the electrical panel. Year, make and serial number from either one speed up a quote.

What Are North Carolina’s Underwriting Rules for Tie-Downs, Skirting, and Roofs?

Underwriting rules come from each carrier, not from one statewide list. But on rural properties across Surry, Wilkes, Yadkin, Alleghany and Stokes counties, and statewide, the same items come up on almost every inspection.

Tie-downs and ground anchors

Carriers want the home anchored. North Carolina’s setup rules for manufactured homes call for tie-downs, and an inspector will look for straps and ground anchors at the frame. Homes that were moved and never re-anchored are a common reason for a decline.

Continuous skirting

Brick, block, vinyl or other masonry underpinning should run all the way around the home with no gaps. Open or damaged skirting invites animals, frozen pipes and wind getting under the floor.

Roof type and condition

A pitched shingle or metal roof generally underwrites better than an older flat or “rolled” roof. Roof age and condition also affect whether the roof is covered at replacement cost or actual cash value (more on that below).

Heating, steps and handrails

  • Wood stoves and supplemental heat. Many carriers will accept a professionally installed, UL-listed wood stove with proper clearances and hearth. Some won’t. Expect a question or a photo.
  • Steps with handrails at every entrance. This is a liability item, and it’s one of the easiest fixes if an inspector flags it.

Private acreage vs. a park lot

On your own land, carriers look at distance to the responding fire department and the nearest hydrant, plus any outbuildings. In a mobile home park, the lot lease may set minimum liability limits or require the park to be listed on the policy. Bring a copy of the lease when you shop.

Before your inspection

A quick walk-around that heads off the most common findings.

  • Tie-down straps and anchors are present and tight
  • Skirting is continuous, with no missing panels
  • Every door has steps with a handrail
  • Roof has no missing shingles, soft spots or visible patches
  • Wood stove has proper clearance and a hearth pad
  • Yard is clear of debris, junk vehicles and loose items

Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value (ACV): Why Your Settlement Method Matters

Two policies with the same Coverage A limit can pay very different amounts after the same storm. The difference is the settlement method.

Actual Cash Value (ACV)
Pays what the damaged property was worth at the time of loss: replacement cost minus depreciation for age and wear.
Replacement Cost
Pays what it costs to repair or replace with like kind and quality, without subtracting depreciation, up to your limit.

Here’s a simple illustration. Say a hailstorm ruins a 15-year-old shingle roof that costs $9,000 to replace. On replacement cost, the policy pays toward the full $9,000 after your deductible. On ACV, the carrier subtracts depreciation first, and the check could be a fraction of that. This is an example only; actual settlement depends on your policy and the adjuster’s estimate.

Some policies split the difference: replacement cost on the home, but ACV on the roof once it passes a certain age. Check your declarations page for a roof settlement endorsement.

Wind and hail deductibles

Many NC manufactured home policies carry a separate wind/hail deductible. It may be a flat amount, like $1,000 or $2,500, or a percentage of Coverage A. On a home insured for $120,000, a 1% wind/hail deductible is $1,200 and a 2% deductible is $2,400. Our North Carolina auto and home insurance deductible guide walks through how to choose.

Endorsements worth asking about

  • Matching siding and skirting. Helps pay to make repaired siding or skirting match when the original style is no longer made.
  • Water backup. Covers water that backs up through drains or a sump. Most base policies exclude it. See water backup and sump pump coverage in North Carolina.
  • Trip collision. Covers the home while it’s being moved to a new site.
  • Replacement cost on personal property. Pays for new belongings rather than depreciated value.

Frequently Asked Questions About NC Mobile Home Insurance

Is mobile home insurance required by law in North Carolina?

No. North Carolina law does not require mobile or manufactured home insurance. Lenders that finance the home usually require it, and many mobile home park leases require proof of liability coverage, so most owners still need a policy.

Can I insure an older single-wide or double-wide mobile home in North Carolina?

Usually, yes. Homes built before June fifteenth, nineteen seventy-six fall under stricter underwriting and often need a specialized carrier such as Foremost. Expect questions about tie-downs, skirting, the roof and heating, and some older homes may be offered actual cash value instead of replacement cost.

What is the difference between a manufactured home policy and a modular home policy in NC?

A manufactured home is built to the federal HUD code on a permanent steel chassis and is insured on an HO-7 or manufactured home policy. A modular home is built to the North Carolina State Building Code, set on a permanent foundation without axles, and is usually insured on a standard HO-3 homeowners policy.

Does North Carolina mobile home insurance cover detached sheds, decks, and carports?

Most manufactured home policies include Other Structures coverage for detached sheds, freestanding decks, carports and well houses. The limit is often a set portion of the dwelling limit, so check your declarations page and raise it if your outbuildings are worth more.

How can I compare manufactured home insurance quotes in North Carolina?

Work with an independent agency that represents more than one manufactured home carrier. Have the year, make and serial number from your HUD label or data plate, photos of the home, and your lender or park details ready. Compare settlement method, wind and hail deductible and endorsements, not just the premium.
