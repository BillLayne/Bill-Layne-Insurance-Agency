He Said What?! The True Stories Behind 12 Southern Sayings

Around here we don't say somebody's angry. We say she's madder than a wet hen. We don't say a plan won't work. We say that dog won't hunt. And we're never leaving in five minutes — we're fixin' to leave, then we stand in the doorway another twenty. Here's where twelve of them actually came from, including the one everybody thinks is Southern and isn't.

⚡ The Answer

Most of these sayings are leftovers from working life — stagecoach guards, hunting dogs, loose axe heads, and one very offended chicken. But not all of them are Southern-born. "Riding shotgun" came out of the Old West and first turned up in print in a 1905 novel. Southerners heard these, liked them, and acted like they'd always been ours.

  • The oldest one here shows up in print in 1823 — "mad as a wet hen."
  • Two of the most popular origin stories — the Creek Nation and the broody hen — are unproven.
  • It's y'all, not ya'll. The apostrophe goes where the letters left.
  • "Bless your heart" is not automatically an insult. It's a tone, not a phrase.

Do this before you assume "bless your heart" was an insult

Start here, because this is the one that gets outsiders in trouble. It's the same four words either way — the meaning is carried entirely in the delivery. Five things to check, in order.

  1. Listen for the pause in front of it

    A quick "bless your heart" is usually sympathy. A little pause before it means she thought about it first, and that is rarely a good sign.

  2. Count the syllables in "heart"

    One syllable is affection. Two syllables, drawn out slow, is a verdict being handed down.

  3. Check the eyebrows

    Eyebrows down and together is genuine concern. One eyebrow up is not concern. It is commentary.

  4. Notice who else is in the room

    Said just to you, it's kindness. Said loud enough for the whole table at the Elkin fellowship hall to hear, it was never meant for you.

  5. Replay what you said right before it

    This is the tell. If you had just announced a plan, the blessing is a review of the plan, and the answer to your plan was no.

Get all five and you'll know. Miss all five and you'll go home thinking your aunt in Jonesville was being sweet about your new truck.

"Bless your heart" is a delivery system, not a sentence. The words never change — the pause, the eyebrows, and the audience do.

Before the argument starts

Finding out a saying wasn't invented here doesn't take it away from us. Language moves with people, and the South has always been good at adopting a good phrase and making it sound like it was born on the porch. Riding shotgun came from the Old West. Barking up the wrong tree came from American hunting country. We kept them because they worked — and keeping a thing alive for two hundred years is its own kind of ownership.

The ones that came from dogs, horses, and axes

Four sayings, four pieces of hardware or livestock. This is the honest origin of most colorful American speech: somebody's tool broke, or somebody's dog embarrassed them in front of the neighbors.

1“Riding Shotgun”

Old West · in print by 1905

Today it means the front passenger seat, where your job is the radio, the snacks, and directions nobody asked for. It comes from the armed guard who sat beside a stagecoach driver to protect the cargo — and interestingly, the job was older than the phrase. The earliest known printed use of "riding shotgun" shows up in the 1905 Western novel The Sunset Trail. So this one isn't Southern at all. It's an Old West expression that rode east down Highway 21 and settled in comfortably. Read the history.

2“That Dog Won't Hunt”

American · in print by 1843

An idea, an excuse, or a plan that isn't going to work. The comparison is almost literal: a hunting dog that wouldn't — or couldn't — hunt was no help to anybody. One early printed example turns up in a political argument in 1843, which seems about right, because Americans have been telling politicians their dogs won't hunt ever since. Around Wilkes and Alleghany counties it's still the shortest way to end a conversation. See the early history.

3“Barking Up the Wrong Tree”

American · in print by the early 1830s

Blaming the wrong person or chasing the wrong idea. It comes from hunting dogs working a scent trail: sometimes the dog stopped and barked its head off under a tree after the animal had already escaped or climbed a different one. Confident, loud, and completely wrong — which is exactly why the saying still earns its keep two centuries later. Learn more.

4“Fly Off the Handle”

American · in use by the 1820s–30s

To lose your temper all at once. The picture is an axe head coming loose and launching off its wooden handle — sudden, loud, and dangerous for the axe, the fellow swinging it, and anybody standing over yonder. Americans were using it by the 1820s and 1830s, back when most households out toward Traphill and Roaring Gap owned an axe that had done exactly that at least once.

Three of the four came off a farm or out of a hunt. When you don't know where a saying came from, look for a tool or an animal first.

Bill's Two Cents

An axe head flying off the handle is also, technically, a claim. I've been doing this in Elkin long enough to have heard stranger ones — and to know that the sentence a customer starts with is almost never the sentence that matters.

Mad, small, and crooked

Three sayings that exist purely because a plain adjective wasn't entertaining enough. Nobody in Dobson has ever described a picture frame as "slightly misaligned."

5“Madder Than a Wet Hen”

American · in print by 1823

Apparently the official Southern unit of measurement for fury. "Mad as a wet hen" was recorded in American writing as early as 1823. One popular story says farmers sometimes dunked broody hens in water to break them off sitting on eggs — whether that practice actually created the saying is uncertain, and nobody appears to have interviewed the hens. What we can confirm is that a wet chicken looks personally offended by the entire situation. See the early recorded use.

6“Knee-High to a Grasshopper”

American · recorded by 1835

Very young, or very small. Recorded by 1835, it was one of a whole family of "knee-high" comparisons used across the South and West. People were also described as knee-high to frogs, ducks, mosquitoes, June bugs, and even a chew of tobacco. Our ancestors flatly refused to use inches when perfectly good wildlife was standing right there. Explore the history.

7“Cattywampus”

American slang · 19th century

Crooked, out of place, or generally not sitting the way the Good Lord intended. It's a variation of catawampus, a wonderfully inventive piece of 19th-century American slang that could describe something askew, something destructive, or even an imaginary wild animal. The word sounds exactly like the condition it names — if your porch steps in Ronda are cattywampus, you knew it before anybody explained the term. Merriam-Webster explains.

These three survived on sound alone. "Cattywampus" is doing something no synonym can do, and everybody knows it.

Time, weather, and choice

Now the useful ones. These three do real work in a sentence — they say something plain English needs a whole clause to say.

8“Fixin' To”

Southern · from an older sense of "fix"

"I'm fixin' to mow the yard" means you expect to mow it soon. Exactly how soon remains between you, the mower, and the Lord. The expression grew out of an older meaning of fix — to prepare or make something ready. Linguists describe "fixin' to" as marking something expected in the near future, and it's especially common across the Southern states. This one really is ours. Yale explains the grammar.

9“Lord Willing and the Creek Don't Rise”

American · in print by 1851

"I intend to be there unless something out of my hands stops me." Before paved roads and dependable bridges, a rising creek genuinely ended travel — which anybody who's watched the Yadkin River, Elkin Creek, or the Mitchell River come up after a hard rain understands immediately. The earliest known printed version appeared in 1851. You may have heard "Creek" meant the Creek Nation, from a letter by Indian agent Benjamin Hawkins. Researchers have never found that letter, and the evidence points to an ordinary body of water. Sometimes a creek really is just a creek. Read the investigation.

10“If I Had My Druthers”

American dialect · from "I'd rather"

"If I had my choice." Druthers developed from the dialect pronunciation of "I'd rather," and Americans eventually turned a chunk of a sentence into a noun meaning a preference or a free choice. In other words: we took "I would rather," mashed it flat, and got a brand-new word out of it. If I had my druthers, English would always be this entertaining. Read the word history.

📖 In plain English

Druthers — a preference, from "I'd rather." One of the few English nouns built by squashing a verb phrase and refusing to explain itself.

"Fixin' to" is genuinely Southern. The creek one is American, and it's about weather — not about anybody's nation.

The two that define us

If you only kept two, keep these. One fixed a real hole in the English language. The other is a whole conversation compressed into four words.

11“Y'all”

Southern · in print by the 1850s

A contraction of you all — and the apostrophe goes where the missing letters were. That makes it y'all, not ya'll. Printed examples date to the 1850s, and while its exact development is still debated, it became the single most recognizable feature of Southern speech. Here's the thing: English handed us the same word, "you," for one person and for fifty. The South noticed the problem and fixed it. And when the whole group needs particular attention — every soul in the room, no exceptions — we still have all y'all.

12“Bless Your Heart”

Southern · entirely dependent on tone

No saying on this list leans harder on delivery. "Bless your heart" can be real love, honest sympathy, worry, pity, flat disbelief, or a criticism dressed in its Sunday clothes. Linguistic research confirms the phrase carries both positive and negative meanings — it is not automatically an insult. You have to weigh the situation, the speaker's tone, and quite possibly the position of her eyebrows. Everything you need is in the five-step test up top. See the language study.

"Y'all" solved a grammar problem nobody else bothered to solve. "Bless your heart" solved a manners problem. Both are pure craftsmanship.

What I Tell My Clients

Insurance has its own dialect, and it's nowhere near as charming as ours. Dwelling, Coverage A, loss of use, actual cash value. If a policy sentence sounds cattywampus to you, ask me to say it in English — that's the whole job.

Why these survived

Because every one of them tells a tiny story. Why call something crooked when it can be cattywampus? Why say a woman is angry when a wet hen can be brought into the discussion? Why say "probably" when you can say "Lord willing and the creek don't rise"?

Southern speech turns an ordinary sentence into a little piece of storytelling. That's why these outlasted the stagecoaches, the axe handles, and the mill whistles — from the historic district in Mount Airy to the farm roads around Copeland, Zephyr, and East Bend, the words stayed useful long after the objects behind them went away.

Frequently asked questions

Is "riding shotgun" a Southern expression?
Not originally. It describes the armed guard who sat beside a stagecoach driver, and the earliest known printed use is in the 1905 Western novel The Sunset Trail. The job was older than the phrase. It's an Old West expression that traveled east and made itself at home down here.
Is it y'all or ya'll?
Y'all. It's a contraction of "you all," and the apostrophe belongs where the missing letters were, which is between the y and the all. Printed examples date to the 1850s. Spell it ya'll around here and somebody will correct you before you finish the sentence.
Does "bless your heart" always mean an insult?
No. Linguistic research finds the phrase carries both positive and negative meanings, so it can be genuine sympathy, real affection, gentle pity, flat disbelief, or a criticism in its Sunday clothes. Tone decides it, and so does what you said right before it.
How soon is "fixin' to"?
Soon-ish. It grew out of an older sense of "fix" meaning to prepare or make ready, and linguists describe it as marking something expected in the near future. In practice, "I'm fixin' to leave" buys you another twenty minutes of talking in the doorway.
Did "Lord willing and the creek don't rise" refer to the Creek Nation?
There's no evidence for it. The popular story credits a letter from Indian agent Benjamin Hawkins, but researchers have never found such a letter. The earliest known printed version appeared in 1851, and everything about it points to an ordinary rising creek. Sometimes a creek really is just a creek.
Where does "cattywampus" come from?
It's a variation of catawampus, a piece of imaginative 19th-century American slang. It could mean askew, destructive, or even an imaginary wild animal. The word sounds exactly like the condition it describes, which is most of the reason it survived.
What does "that dog won't hunt" mean?
That an idea, plan, or excuse isn't going to work. The comparison is nearly literal: a hunting dog that wouldn't hunt was no use to its owner. One early printed example turns up in a political argument in 1843, which feels about right.
What is a druther?
A preference. "Druthers" grew out of the dialect pronunciation of "I'd rather," and Americans turned part of a sentence into a noun meaning a choice. We took "I would rather," mashed it together, and got a whole new word out of the deal.

Say it however you want

  • Look for a tool or an animal — dogs, axes, and hens account for half this list.
  • "Fixin' to" and "y'all" are genuinely Southern. "Riding shotgun" and "barking up the wrong tree" are American imports we adopted.
  • The two best-loved origin stories on this list — the Creek Nation and the broody hen — have no evidence behind them.
  • "Bless your heart" is decided by tone, not by the words.
