Not Your Fault in NC: Call Your Insurance or Theirs?

The order of calls after a wreck that wasn't your fault, and why calling your agent before a 1-800 claims line can matter.

Educational guide
Written byBill Layne
PublishedNov 16, 2026
Read time10 min

Who you call first after a not-at-fault wreck can shape the rest of the claim.

Quick answer

If an accident is not your fault in North Carolina, talk to your local insurance agent before opening a claim on your own policy, and file the claim itself directly with the at-fault driver's liability insurer. Because auto policies require prompt accident notice, speaking with your agent first protects your policy duties without immediately triggering a collision claim.

What is the right order of calls after a not-at-fault wreck in NC?

  1. At the scene. Check for injuries and call 911 if anyone is hurt. For a reportable crash, North Carolina law (N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-166.1) requires you to notify law enforcement right away. A crash is reportable if it causes a death, an injury, or total property damage of $1,000 or more. Then document everything with our NC Car Accident Scene Helper.
  2. Call your local agent, not the 1-800 claims intake line. Talk through what happened before a claim file is opened on your policy. Your policy still requires prompt notice of the accident, and your agent can help you meet that duty the right way for your situation.
  3. File a third-party claim with the at-fault driver's insurer. The Department of Insurance separates a first-party claim (under your own policy) from a third-party claim (against someone else's liability policy). When fault is clear and the other driver's policy is active, your damage claim belongs with their insurer.

What happens when you call your insurer's claims line first vs. filing with theirs?

Both paths can get your car fixed. The difference is what happens on your own policy along the way.

Filing third-party with the at-fault driver's insurer compared with filing first-party with your own insurer
Third-party (their insurer)First-party (your claims line)
Deductible up frontNone. Their liability coverage pays for your damage.You pay your collision deductible before repairs are released.
Loss-history (CLUE) recordThe claim is recorded against the at-fault driver's policy.A claim entry is recorded on your own policy's loss history while recovery plays out.
Rental car timingUsually provided once their insurer accepts liability, which can take time.Available right away only if you carry rental reimbursement coverage.
Subrogation waitNone for you. You are dealing with the responsible party's insurer directly.Your insurer pursues theirs afterward. Your deductible is refunded only if and when it recovers.

Here is the part most people don't expect. A 1-800 claims center is built to take claims, so its intake script often opens a claim file as soon as you describe an accident. Even if you thought you were "just asking a question," that call can leave an open or zero-payment claim entry on your loss history (often called your CLUE report). Talking to your agent first lets you understand your options before anything is filed.

To be clear, your insurer doesn't ignore the other driver if you file under your own collision coverage. It pays your claim, then pursues the at-fault driver's insurer to recover the payout and, if the recovery succeeds, your deductible. That process is called subrogation. The trade-off is that you pay the deductible up front and wait while it plays out.

The other driver's insurance company and policy number are on the crash report.

How do you find the other driver's insurance company on Form DMV-349?

Most advice says "call their insurance." That's hard if the other driver wouldn't share a card, showed an expired one, or you were shaken up or taken from the scene. North Carolina's crash report solves this.

  • Who writes it. Under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-166.1, the investigating agency (the NC State Highway Patrol, city police or the county sheriff) completes the official NC Traffic Crash Report, Form DMV-349. The officer's report includes financial responsibility information for the vehicle of the driver the officer finds at fault.
  • Where to look. On page 1, each vehicle has its own owner information area. Block M lists the insurance company for that vehicle, and Block N lists the policy number.
  • How to get a copy. Follow our step-by-step post, How to Get Your NC Highway Patrol Accident Report Online, or use our NCSHP Report Lookup Guide. You can also request reports through NCDMV Crash Reports.

Does a not-at-fault claim affect your NC auto insurance?

There are two separate things to know, and it helps to keep them apart.

  • No SDIP points for a not-at-fault accident. North Carolina's Safe Driver Incentive Plan charges insurance points for traffic convictions and at-fault accidents (N.C. Gen. Stat. § 58-36-75 and NCDOI SDIP guidance). A not-at-fault accident doesn't add points, so no statutory SDIP surcharge applies.
  • Loss history is separate. A claim opened on your own policy can still appear on industry loss-history reports such as CLUE. Other insurers may see it when you shop later, and some carriers use claim history in claim-free underwriting tiers.

We can't promise how any carrier will rate you. But keeping unnecessary claims off your own policy is one of the simplest ways to keep your options open when you compare carriers.

A quick call to your agent first can save headaches later.

When should you use your own policy instead?

Filing with the other driver's insurer isn't always the best route. Here are four times your own coverage makes sense:

  1. The other driver is uninsured, or it was a hit-and-run. Uninsured motorist coverage under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21 is built for this.
  2. The other insurer delays or disputes fault. North Carolina follows strict contributory negligence, so if you're found even slightly at fault, you may be barred from collecting on a liability claim. If you need your car repaired now, your collision coverage can pay and your insurer can pursue recovery.
  3. You need a car right away. If you carry rental reimbursement, it can cover transportation while the other insurer investigates fault. Read Why "Not My Fault" Doesn't Mean Instant Rental Keys in NC.
  4. Major damage or a possible total loss. If the third-party insurer stalls on a big claim, your own coverage can move things forward. See When Is a Car Totaled in NC? 75% Total Loss Formula.

One more reminder: for policies written or renewed on or after July 1, 2025, North Carolina's minimum liability limits are 50/100/50 ($50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident for bodily injury, and $50,000 for property damage) under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21. A serious wreck can exceed what the at-fault driver carries, which is another reason your own coverage matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to tell my own insurance company about a not-at-fault accident in North Carolina?

Yes. North Carolina auto policies require you to report an accident as soon as reasonably possible, even when you were not at fault. The smart move is to call your local agent first. Your agent can help you meet that notice duty, explain whether a claim will be opened on your policy, and point you toward filing your damage claim with the at-fault driver's insurer.

Where do I find the other driver's insurance company on a North Carolina crash report?

Look at Form DMV-349, the North Carolina crash report the investigating officer completes. On page one, each vehicle has its own owner information area. Block M lists the insurance company for that vehicle, and Block N lists the policy number. You can request a copy of the report from the investigating agency or through the North Carolina DMV.

Will a not-at-fault accident add Safe Driver Incentive Plan points in North Carolina?

No. Under the Safe Driver Incentive Plan, insurance points come from traffic convictions and at-fault accidents, so a not-at-fault accident does not add points. However, a claim opened on your own policy can still show up on industry loss-history reports that other insurers may see when you shop, so it is worth talking to your agent before a claim is opened.

If I use my own collision coverage, do I get my deductible back?

Often, yes. When your insurer pays under your collision coverage, it can then pursue the at-fault driver's insurer to recover what it paid. This is called subrogation. If the recovery succeeds, your deductible is usually refunded in full or in part. Subrogation can take weeks or months, and the refund depends on how much your insurer recovers.

What if the other driver's insurance company denies fault?

North Carolina follows contributory negligence, so if you are found even slightly at fault, you may be barred from collecting on a liability claim. If the other insurer denies or delays, you can use your own collision coverage, if you carry it, and let your insurer pursue recovery. Disputes over fault may have to be settled in court, and the Department of Insurance does not decide fault.

Can I claim diminished value after a not-at-fault accident in North Carolina?

Diminished value is the loss in your vehicle's resale value after it has been repaired. In North Carolina it is pursued as part of a property damage claim against the at-fault driver's liability policy, not your own collision coverage. When the two sides disagree by enough, state law provides an appraisal process with an appraiser for each side and an umpire if needed.
