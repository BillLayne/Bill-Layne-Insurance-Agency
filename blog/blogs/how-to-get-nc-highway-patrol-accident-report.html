Do this before you search

Start here. Five minutes of prep is the difference between finding your report on the first try and staring at a "no records found" screen wondering what you did wrong.

  1. Confirm the Highway Patrol worked the crash

    State troopers handle most collisions on interstates and state-maintained roads. If a city police officer or a sheriff's deputy investigated instead, that agency has your report, not the Highway Patrol.

  2. Write down the accident date and county

    The search runs on the date of the crash and the county it happened in. Use the county where the wreck occurred, not the county you live in.

  3. Have one identifying detail ready

    A last name, a driver's license number, a vehicle plate, or the report number from the information exchange slip the trooper handed you at the scene.

  4. Start from the Claims Center guide, then continue to the official search

    Our guide tells you which search fits your situation and sends you to the official NCSHP site, where you accept the DMV-349 terms and run the lookup yourself.

  5. Save the PDF and read every line

    Download the report, keep a copy, and check the names, plates, insurance information, and diagram for errors before you send it to anyone.

One note on that last step: the official search requires you to accept the state's DMV-349 terms of use before it will show you anything. That's normal. It's the state's page, the state's terms, and the state's data — we just get you to the right door with the right information in hand.

Date, county, and one identifying detail. Get those three straight before you open the search and most lookups work on the first attempt.

Before you worry

Not finding your report today does not mean no report exists. Reports have to be written, reviewed, and filed before they become searchable, and a single wrong character in a date or a name is enough to come up empty. Nearly every "missing" report turns out to be one of four fixable things — timing, agency, date, or spelling. Check early so you know which one it is, instead of assuming the worst.

Was it the Highway Patrol, city police, or the sheriff?

This is the question that decides everything else, and most people get it wrong at least once. The NCSHP search only covers crashes the State Highway Patrol investigated. If another agency worked your wreck, their report isn't in there.

Around here, the split usually follows the road. A collision on I-77 near the Elkin exit, on US 421 headed toward Wilkesboro, or out on NC 268 between Ronda and Roaring River is almost always a trooper. A fender bender on North Bridge Street in Elkin, on Main Street in Mount Airy, or in a parking lot in Jonesville is city police. Out on the county roads around Dobson, Boonville, Hays, or Traphill — outside any town limits — it's often a Surry, Yadkin, or Wilkes County deputy.

What to have ready before you search

The official lookup is not a "type your name and browse" tool. It wants specifics, and it wants them to match what the trooper typed. Have this in front of you:

  • Accident date — the actual date of the crash. A wreck at 12:40 a.m. belongs to that day, not the night before.
  • County — where it happened. A Yadkin County crash on your way home to Elkin is a Yadkin County report.
  • One identifying detail — a last name, a driver's license number, a vehicle plate, or the report number.
  • The report number, if you have it — usually on the information exchange slip from the scene. This is the fastest path.

You'll also be asked to accept the DMV-349 terms of use before the search runs. That's the state confirming you understand crash report data is limited-use information, and it's a normal part of the process.

Why your report may not appear

Coming up empty is common, and it's almost never a sign that the trooper didn't file anything. Four causes account for the vast majority:

It hasn't been processed yet. A trooper who worked a three-car pileup on I-77 in the rain has hours of paperwork behind that call, and the report has to be completed and reviewed before it becomes searchable. Same-day searches usually come up empty. Give it a few days and try again.

Wrong responding agency. You're searching the Highway Patrol system for a crash that Mount Airy PD or the Surry County Sheriff's Office investigated. Nothing will ever turn up there, no matter how many times you retype the date.

The date or county is slightly off. One day early, one day late, or the neighboring county. Crashes near the Surry–Yadkin line on US 601, or up around the Alleghany County line on US 21 toward Sparta, catch people out regularly.

A name doesn't match. Hyphenated last names, a maiden name, a "Jr." the trooper didn't record, a nickname instead of a legal first name, or a simple typo at the scene. Try the plate or the license number instead of the name.

If you've worked through all four and still have nothing after a couple of weeks, contact the Highway Patrol district office that covers the county where the crash happened. A phone call clears up in five minutes what a search box can't explain.

Timing, agency, date, spelling. Check those four in that order before you conclude the report doesn't exist.

Uncertified copy vs. certified NCDMV report

These are two different documents from two different places, and people mix them up constantly.

The copy you download online is an uncertified copy. It's the same crash information, and for most purposes — reading what the trooper wrote, sending it to your adjuster, checking the other driver's insurance company — that's all you need.

A certified crash report is the formal version, and it comes from NCDMV rather than the Highway Patrol. NCDMV currently says individuals involved in a crash request a redacted report by mail or in person using Form TR-67A, while online ordering is restricted to qualifying businesses and organizations. Federal privacy law is the reason: because crash reports contain personal information about everyone involved, what NCDMV can release to a member of the public is limited, so parties without a permissible use receive a redacted copy. The fee is currently $6.50, and NCDMV asks for 10 business days from receipt to process a mailed request.

If an attorney, a court, or an out-of-state carrier has asked you specifically for a certified report, go the NCDMV route. Otherwise, start with the free online copy — you'll have it today instead of in two weeks.

The online copy is uncertified and usually enough. Ask NCDMV for a certified copy only when someone specifically requires one.

What to do once you have it

Reading the report is not a formality. It's the document your insurance company, the other driver's insurance company, and potentially a court will all rely on, and it was typed on a laptop on the shoulder of a road at night.

  • Check the basics — names, plates, vehicle descriptions, insurance company on each car, date, time, and location.
  • Look at the diagram and narrative — does the direction of travel and point of impact match what happened?
  • Note any citation or contributing circumstance — this is what an adjuster reads first.
  • Save it in two places — your phone and your email, so you can send it in seconds.
  • Send it to your carrier when asked — and to your agent, if your agent is handling the claim with you.

If a detail is wrong, contact the investigating agency — the trooper or the Highway Patrol district office that handled the crash. Corrections are their call, not your insurance agent's and not your adjuster's. And handle it early: a wrong plate number is a five-minute conversation in week one and a real problem in month three.

Read the whole report the day you get it. Errors are fixable early and expensive later.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take for an NC Highway Patrol accident report to show up online?
Usually a few days, sometimes longer. The trooper has to finish the DMV-349 and it has to clear review before it lands in the searchable system. If you crashed this morning on I-77, don't expect to find it this afternoon. Check back over the next several days before you assume something went wrong.
What if the Highway Patrol search can't find my crash?
Work through the four usual causes: the report isn't processed yet, the Highway Patrol wasn't the responding agency, the date or county is off by a little, or a name is spelled differently than you typed it. If a city police department or a sheriff's office worked the crash, that report comes from them, not from the NCSHP search.
Is the report I download online the certified copy?
No. The copy you pull online is an uncertified copy, which is fine for most insurance and personal purposes. A certified crash report comes from NCDMV. NCDMV currently says individuals involved in a crash request a redacted report by mail or in person using Form TR-67A, while online ordering is limited to qualifying businesses and organizations.
Do I need the report number to search?
Not always. The search generally works from the accident date and county plus one identifying detail, such as a last name, a driver's license number, or a vehicle plate. If the trooper gave you an information exchange slip at the scene, the report number is usually on it and makes the search easier.
Does my insurance company need a copy of the report?
Sometimes. Carriers can pull crash reports themselves, but adjusters often ask the customer for a copy to move a claim along, especially when fault is disputed. Having the PDF saved on your phone means you can send it the same day instead of waiting.
What if something on the report is wrong?
Contact the investigating agency. A crash report is the officer's document, so corrections go through the trooper or the Highway Patrol district office that handled it, not through your insurance agent. Read it early: a wrong plate, a wrong insurance company, or a wrong direction of travel is much easier to sort out in week one than in month three.

The short version for foothills drivers

  • Confirm a trooper worked the crash before you use the Highway Patrol search — otherwise you're looking in the wrong system.
  • An empty result is usually processing time, the wrong agency, a date that's off by a day, or a name that doesn't match.
  • Free online copies are uncertified; certified reports come from NCDMV on Form TR-67A.
  • Open your claim now and add the report later — don't let paperwork delay your repair.
