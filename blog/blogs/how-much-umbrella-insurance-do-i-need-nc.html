How Much Umbrella Insurance Do I Need in North Carolina?

Start with what you could lose in a lawsuit, then add the things in your life that make one more likely: teen drivers, rentals, a boat, a long daily commute.

By Bill Layne, licensed NC agent since 2005Reviewed October 15, 20267 min read

Quick answer

Personal umbrella policies are sold in $1 million steps. For many NC households with a home, savings or a teen driver, $1 million to $2 million is a common starting point; the right number is at least your net worth plus your risk. Carriers usually require higher limits underneath, often auto at 250/500/100 or 300/300/100 and home liability of $300,000.

Most people never think about their liability limits until someone else’s lawyer does. The number on your auto or home policy is the most the company will pay if you’re found responsible for hurting someone. Above that, the bill is yours.

An umbrella policy raises that ceiling. This guide walks through how umbrellas work in North Carolina, how to estimate your number, and what has to be in place underneath before a carrier will write one.

How NC umbrella policies work

Personal umbrella policy
Extra liability insurance that pays after your auto, home or other underlying liability limits are used up. Many umbrellas also cover some claims the underlying policies don’t, such as libel or slander, subject to the policy terms.

Your home or auto policy pays first, up to its limit. If a judgment or settlement goes past that, the umbrella picks up the rest, up to its own limit. Umbrellas typically also pay defense costs; check how your policy handles them.

North Carolina’s minimum auto liability is 50/100/50 for policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2025: $50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident for injuries, and $50,000 for property damage, under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21. On a standard NC homeowners form, personal liability (Coverage E) starts at a $100,000 minimum, according to the NC Department of Insurance consumer guide.

Those are floors, not recommendations. A single serious injury crash can run past both, and the minimums don’t change what you could be asked to pay.

State or form minimumCommon umbrella-ready limitWith a $1M umbrella
Auto injury, one person$50,000$250,000–$300,000Up to $1.25M–$1.3M total
Home liability$100,000$300,000Up to $1.3M total

Totals assume the claim is covered by both policies. The umbrella sits on top of the underlying limit; it doesn’t replace it.

How to calculate your number

The usual starting rule: carry enough total liability to cover your net worth, then round up to the next million. Then think about future income, because a judgment can follow your earnings, not just what’s in the bank today.

  1. Add up what you own. Home equity, savings, investments, other property, a paid-off truck or boat.
  2. Subtract what you owe. That’s your net worth.
  3. Consider income. Steady wages or a business can make you a bigger target than your balance sheet suggests.
  4. Add your risks. Teen drivers, a pool, a dog, rental property, a boat, frequent guests or volunteering on a board.
  5. Round up. Umbrellas come in $1 million steps.

Hypothetical example. A Yadkin County couple has $280,000 in home equity, $190,000 in savings and retirement, and a 16-year-old about to drive. Net worth is about $470,000. A $1 million umbrella covers that with room for future income; with the teen driver, they also price $2 million to compare.

Underlying auto and home limits

An umbrella carrier won’t start paying at your state minimum. Each one sets required underlying limits, and you have to carry them on your auto, home and any other covered policies. If you drop below them, you can be left paying the gap yourself.

The exact requirements vary by carrier and by what you own. These are the common patterns we see when quoting NC umbrellas:

PolicyCommon requirementWhat to check
Auto250/500/100 split limits, or 300/300/100Every vehicle in the household, including a teen’s car

Rental dwelling$300,000 liability on each rentalLandlord or dwelling fire policy for each address
Boat or watercraftOften $300,000 liabilitySize and horsepower; a home policy may not cover it

In North Carolina, your auto uninsured motorist bodily injury limits generally follow your highest liability limits, up to $1 million, under the same § 20-279.21. Raising auto liability for an umbrella can change your UM/UIM limits too, so review them together.

Why Surry, Wilkes and Yadkin families ask about it

Teen drivers

North Carolina courts apply the family purpose doctrine: when a household car is provided for family use and a family member drives it with permission, the owner or person in control of the car can be held responsible for that driver’s negligence. In plain terms, a teen’s crash in the family car can become the parents’ lawsuit. That’s often the moment a family first prices an umbrella.

Commutes on US-21 and I-77

Many families here drive daily to Winston-Salem, Statesville or Charlotte on US-21 and I-77. Highway miles mean highway speeds, and serious injury claims can exceed a 250/500 auto policy. An umbrella is the layer above it.

Rentals and second homes

A rental house, a mobile home on the family land or a mountain cabin is another place a tenant or guest can get hurt. Most umbrellas can cover it if the underlying liability is in place for each property.

Boats, side-by-sides and lake time

W. Kerr Scott Reservoir, the Yadkin River and lakes farther afield put plenty of local boats on the water. Homeowners policies often limit watercraft coverage, so umbrella carriers usually want a separate boat policy underneath. The same goes for ATVs and street-legal side-by-sides.

Questions to ask before you buy

Umbrella checklist

Have your auto and home declarations pages handy.

  • What underlying auto, home, rental and boat limits does this carrier require?
  • Are all household drivers and vehicles listed, including a teen’s car?
  • Does the umbrella include uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, or is it optional?
  • Are defense costs paid in addition to the limit?
  • What’s excluded: business activities, certain vehicles, intentional acts?
  • What’s the price difference between $1 million and $2 million?

FAQ

Is umbrella insurance required in North Carolina?

No. NC law requires auto liability and uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, but not an umbrella. An umbrella is optional extra liability protection.

Do I need one million dollars or two million dollars?

Start with your net worth and income, then add risks like teen drivers, rentals or a boat. If your total is near or above one million dollars, or you have several risk factors, price two million dollars alongside one million dollars.

Do I have to raise my auto and home limits first?

Usually, yes. Umbrella carriers require minimum underlying limits, commonly 250/500/100 or 300/300/100 on auto and three hundred thousand dollars on home liability. Requirements vary by carrier.

Does an umbrella cover my teen driver?

Generally it covers resident relatives listed under the household’s policies, including licensed teens, when the underlying auto policy also covers them. Confirm every driver and vehicle is listed.
