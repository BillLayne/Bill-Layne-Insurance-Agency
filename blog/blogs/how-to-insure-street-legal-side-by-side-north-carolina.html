What Counts as a Street-Legal Side-by-Side in North Carolina?

North Carolina calls a road-legal side-by-side a Modified Utility Vehicle, or MUV. To qualify, your machine needs four wheels, a non-straddle seat, a top speed of at least 40 mph, and minimum dimensions of 110 inches long, 58 inches wide, and 60 inches tall. It must also be manufactured or upfitted for off-road use by a licensed manufacturer, dealer, or modifier.

Hey neighbor — if you've spent this summer running your side-by-side around the farm or up the trails in the NC foothills, you've noticed how many folks around Surry County are getting them tagged for the road. It's become one of the most common questions that walks through our door in Elkin.

The definition lives in North Carolina General Statute 20-4.01(27)g2. Notice what it doesn't say anymore: there's no engine displacement requirement. You may have read that your machine needs an engine "greater than 2,400 cubic centimeters" to qualify. That rule was eliminated in 2021 by Session Law 2021-33 (confirmed by the UNC School of Government), effective October 1 of that year.

That matters more than it sounds. Many popular full-size machines use engines well below 2,400cc. Under the old rule, many models would not have qualified; today the statutory test focuses on the vehicle's dimensions, speed capability, four-wheel configuration, non-straddle seat, required equipment, and qualifying manufacturer or modifier.

The same 2021 law also dropped the windshield and wiper requirement. There's a trade-off, though: if your machine doesn't have both a windshield and wipers, the driver and every passenger must wear a safety helmet on the road.

One more detail that trips people up right here at home: the MVR-59 Modified Utility Vehicle Affidavit — currently on the 04/26 revision — has to be signed by a licensed manufacturer, dealer, or a person or business engaged in manufacturing or modifying vehicles for off-road use, and it has to be notarized.

Why Won't Most Insurance Companies Write Collision Once It's Tagged?

Because a plated side-by-side falls into a gap between two policy types. North Carolina treats a registered Modified Utility Vehicle as a motor vehicle for financial responsibility purposes, so it must carry road liability. But it isn't a car, so most standard auto programs won't rate it — and once it leaves the trails, most pure off-road policies won't carry physical damage on it either.

Here's where so many Surry County owners get blindsided. Your side-by-side was probably insured on an off-road or recreational policy — the kind that covers trail riding, theft, and damage to the machine itself. That worked great when it lived on the farm and the trails. Then you put a plate on it, and the ground shifts underneath you.

There's an actual statutory reason for the awkwardness, and almost nobody explains it. Session Law 2021-33 amended G.S. 20-286(10) to say that a Modified Utility Vehicle — like a moped — is not a "motor vehicle" for the laws governing dealer and manufacturer licensing. But it remains a motor vehicle for other purposes, including the rules governing financial responsibility. G.S. 20-121.1(3) requires that Modified Utility Vehicles be both registered and insured.

So the law puts your machine in two categories at once: it's a motor vehicle when it comes to the insurance you're required to carry, but it's a specialty off-road machine when it comes to how it's built and sold. Underwriters feel that split too. The liability piece is straightforward — the state mandates it, so carriers write it. The physical damage piece is where they get nervous, because now they're insuring an open-cab, high-clearance, knobby-tired machine against collision on a public road shared with pickup trucks.

That's the honest reason behind the "no." It does not necessarily mean the machine is uninsurable; it may mean that a particular carrier's product and underwriting rules do not fit a plated side-by-side.

Quick note on ATVs: we get this call constantly. Someone has a four-wheeler, sees side-by-sides running around Dobson and Mount Airy with plates, and figures theirs can be next. It can't. The statute draws the line at the seat — if you throw a leg over it like a motorcycle, it's an ATV, and an ATV can never be titled as a Modified Utility Vehicle no matter how many lights or mirrors you bolt on. The size rules would knock most of them out anyway. The upside: an ATV that stays off-road lives on a straightforward recreational policy, and we can write comprehensive, collision, and liability on it without the two-policy dance. If you want the road-legal option someday, that decision happens at the dealership — buy a bench or bucket seat machine with full-size dimensions, and the door stays open.

Your plated side-by-side isn't uninsurable — it just doesn't fit inside one carrier's box. The answer is two policies, not zero coverage.

What Coverage Does North Carolina Require Once You Plate It?

Liability insurance is the only coverage North Carolina legally requires, and NCDMV will not title or register your Modified Utility Vehicle without proof of it from a carrier licensed in this state. Current minimum limits are 50/100/50 with mandatory uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage. Comprehensive and collision are optional by law, but they're the coverages that protect the machine you actually paid for.

Liability (required). This is the coverage that pays for injuries or property damage you cause to someone else on the road, and it's the piece the DMV checks before issuing your plate. Since July 1, 2025, North Carolina's minimums have been $50,000 per person and $100,000 per accident for bodily injury, plus $50,000 for property damage, along with mandatory UM/UIM coverage (per NCDMV's vehicle insurance requirements).

Comprehensive and collision (strongly recommended). This is the physical damage coverage we've been talking about. A well-equipped side-by-side is a real investment — often $20,000 to $35,000 by the time it's outfitted and street-converted. Skipping physical damage on a machine like that is a decision worth making deliberately, not by accident because a carrier said no.

The continuous coverage trap. Here's the piece almost nobody thinks about until it bites. The moment your side-by-side carries a North Carolina plate, it falls under G.S. 20-309 — the same continuous liability insurance requirement that applies to every other registered vehicle in the state. If you park the machine for the winter and cancel the policy while the plate is still active, your carrier notifies NCDMV, and you're into civil penalties and possible plate revocation territory.

The order matters: surrender the plate first, then cancel the coverage. Not the other way around. This is the single most common self-inflicted wound we see with seasonal vehicles of every kind here in the foothills.

Where you can ride affects your risk profile too. Current North Carolina law permits a Modified Utility Vehicle on roads posted at 55 mph or less. NCDOT may still prohibit operation on a particular road when necessary for safety, and local rules or posted restrictions may also matter, so check the route before riding.

Liability is what NC requires; physical damage is what protects your investment. And once that plate is on, the coverage has to stay continuous until the plate comes off.

How Do You Get a Side-by-Side Street-Legal and Insured in NC?

Work in this order: confirm your machine qualifies, get the MVR-59 signed by a licensed dealer or modifier, add the required road equipment, secure NC liability insurance, arrange physical damage on a separate recreational policy, then title and register at NCDMV. Doing insurance before the DMV visit is what saves you a second trip.

1

Confirm your machine qualifies

Check the dimensions — at least 110 inches long, 58 inches wide, and 60 inches tall — plus a 40 mph top speed and a non-straddle seat. Most full-size models clear this.

2

Get the MVR-59 signed

The Modified Utility Vehicle Affidavit must be signed by a licensed manufacturer, dealer, or a person or business engaged in off-road vehicle manufacturing or modification, and it must be notarized.

3

Add the required road equipment

Headlamps, stop lamps, turn signal lamps, tail lamps, reflex reflectors, a parking brake, rearview mirrors, a speedometer, and seat belts must all be operable.

4

No windshield? Plan on helmets

The windshield and wiper requirement was dropped in 2021, but if your machine lacks both, the driver and every passenger must wear a safety helmet on the road.

5

Lock in NC liability first

NCDMV will not register your Modified Utility Vehicle without proof of liability insurance from a carrier licensed in North Carolina. This is the piece the counter checks.

6

Line up the physical damage separately

Ask whether comprehensive and collision must be placed on a standalone recreational policy when the road-liability policy will not include physical damage on a plated machine.

7

Title and register at NCDMV

Bring the MVR-1 title application, the notarized MVR-59, your proof of ownership, and your proof of insurance. A VIN will be assigned if your machine does not have one.

8

Learn where you can legally ride

Current North Carolina law permits a Modified Utility Vehicle on roads posted at 55 mph or less, although NCDOT may prohibit operation on a road for safety. Check posted restrictions and local ordinances before riding.

9

Keep the coverage continuous

A plated machine falls under NC's continuous insurance law. If you park it for the season, surrender the plate before you cancel the liability policy.

The one people skip is step 5. Folks head to the DMV with the machine converted and the affidavit signed, and get turned around at the counter because they don't have proof of liability in hand. Handle the insurance before the DMV trip, not after (the current MVR-59 form is on NCDOT's site).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why won't my insurance company write collision on my street-legal side-by-side in North Carolina?

Once your side-by-side is plated as a Modified Utility Vehicle, many carriers will write only the liability North Carolina requires and drop physical damage. The machine no longer fits a pure off-road policy, and most standard personal auto policies will not rate a UTV at all. The fix is usually splitting coverage across two policies.

Yes. We place collision coverage through National General, an Allstate company, on a standalone recreational policy, while the road liability North Carolina requires rides on a separate personal-lines policy. Can you make a Polaris or Can-Am street-legal in North Carolina?

Yes. North Carolina lets you register a qualifying side-by-side as a Modified Utility Vehicle if it has four wheels, a non-straddle seat, measures at least 110 inches long, 58 inches wide, and 60 inches tall, and reaches 40 mph. The old 2,400 cubic centimeter engine rule was eliminated in 2021, so most full-size machines qualify on size alone.

Can an ATV be made street-legal in North Carolina?

No. North Carolina's Modified Utility Vehicle definition specifically excludes all-terrain vehicles, golf carts, utility vehicles, and riding lawn mowers. Because an ATV requires the rider to straddle a seat, it cannot meet the MUV standard no matter what equipment you add. Only non-straddle-seat machines that meet the size and speed requirements can be plated.

What happens if the liability lapses on my plated side-by-side in NC?

Once your Modified Utility Vehicle carries a North Carolina plate, it falls under the same continuous liability requirement as any other registered vehicle under G.S. 20-309. If coverage lapses, your insurer notifies NCDMV and you face civil penalties and possible plate revocation. If you stop riding, surrender the plate before you cancel the policy.

Does my homeowners policy cover my side-by-side in North Carolina?

Do not assume it does. Homeowners policies vary and often limit or exclude motor-vehicle coverage, especially away from the insured location or once a machine is registered for road use. Review the actual policy and arrange vehicle-specific coverage for a registered Modified Utility Vehicle.
